Common Causes, Measurement Error, and the Search for Retinal Biomarkers of Brain Disease

The retina is often described as a “window to the brain”. Yet in practice, promising retinal imaging biomarkers frequently appear statistically unassociated with brain disease once researchers adjust for covariates like age in a standard regression model. This interactive guide explores why eye and brain measures correlate, how measurement error on both sides of the equation interacts with covariate adjustment, and what an ideal retinal biomarker looks like in both engineering and causal terms.

Part 1 · Causal Structure & Analogy

Why Would a Retinal Measure Correlate with Brain Disease at All?

In almost all neurovascular studies, narrowing or leakage in a retinal arteriole does not physically cause white matter hyperintensities (WMH) in the cerebral hemispheres, nor do cerebral WMH typically cause microvascular lesions in the retina. There is no direct causal arrow from retina to brain (R* ↛ B*).

Instead, retinal biomarkers rely on a biological analogy grounded in Reichenbach’s Common Cause Principle: when two variables are genuinely associated and neither causes the other, their association arises from one or more shared upstream causes—creating a fork (R* ← Common Cause → B*).

Empirical Anchor · Retinopathy & Severe WMH (Dumitrascu et al., 2018)

In a meta-analysis of 28 prospective studies (n = 56,379), clinically visible retinopathy (adjusted OR 1.94, 95% CI 1.36–2.76) and retinal haemorrhages (adjusted OR 2.23, 95% CI 1.34–3.70) were consistently associated with cerebral WMH even after adjusting for age, hypertension, diabetes, and smoking.

On a causal graph, an association between retina and brain that survives adjustment for measured risk factors (C) implies an unblocked path through a latent microvascular disease process (U*)—such as shared blood–retinal and blood–brain barrier dysfunction—that routine clinic risk factors do not fully capture.

Crucial Caveat · Conditioning on Disease Severity Itself

Try Scenario 1B above, where a single factor causes both retinal and brain changes. If that factor were a direct measure of the very disease process you want your biomarker to detect (for example, stratifying an Alzheimer’s cohort tightly by cognitive severity, or adjusting for a global small-vessel disease score), conditioning on it screens off the fork and makes even a perfect retinal biomarker look useless (MacCormick et al., 2022). Adjusting for a variable is never automatically “good” or “bad”—it always asks whether paths other than the adjusted variable remain open.

Optional Deeper Dive · Structural equations & Reichenbach’s screening-off formula

In Part 1, true retinal and brain states are generated by two independent standardized factors—known risk factor C* ∼ N(0,1) and latent microvascular pathology U* ∼ N(0,1)—plus independent local biological variation ζR, ζB ∼ N(0, σζ2):

R* = αR C* + λR U* + ζR,   B* = αB C* + λB U* + ζB

By the law of total covariance, the overall covariance between R* and B* splits cleanly into the covariance transmitted through C* (αRαB) plus the within-C* covariance transmitted through U* (λRλB). Adjusting for exact C = C* removes αRαB and isolates λRλB. When λR = 0 or λB = 0, C* screens off R* from B* (R* ⊥ B* | C*).

Part 2 · Errors-in-Variables & The SNR Spectrum

Error on Both Sides of the Equation

If Dumitrascu et al. (2018) found clear risk-factor-adjusted associations for frank retinopathy and retinal haemorrhages, why do studies of continuous, early-stage retinal vessel metrics in typical clinical cohorts (n = 80–300) so often report weak or inconsistent results?

Frank haemorrhages are late-stage binary events (“the tip of the iceberg”) pooled across tens of thousands of patients. When we try to measure subtle continuous physiology earlier in the disease course, we confront measurement error on both sides of the regression equation:

  • Error in the retinal predictor (R = R* + ϵR): Optical resolution limits (e.g., sub-pixel vessel calibre changes), cardiac-cycle pulsatility in single snapshots, focus/illumination drift, or media opacity. Because ordinary regression treats the horizontal axis (R) as if it were error-free, horizontal noise stretches the cloud sideways and systematically flattens the regression slope toward zero (attenuation bias).
  • Error in the brain outcome (B = B* + ϵB): Scanner noise, WMH segmentation thresholds, or physiological day-to-day vascular variability. Vertical noise does not bias the linear slope β on average, but it inflates scatter, shrinks the correlation r, and widens confidence intervals.

Optional Deeper Dive · Attenuation equations & why longitudinal change scores are doubly sensitive

Suppose R = R* + ēR (after averaging k repeats within an eye, so Var(ēR) = σϵR2 / k) and B = B* + ϵB, with independent random errors:

ICCR = Var(R*)Var(R*) + σϵR2 / k,   E[β̂OLS] = βtrue · ICCR,   robs ≈ rtrue √ICCR · ICCB

Longitudinal change scores (ΔR = R2 − R1): Subtracting baseline from follow-up cancels out between-person differences, leaving often modest true progression variance (σΔ2), while adding the measurement error variances of both visits (2σϵR2/k). Without high repeatability (k-averaging, video rather than single static frames) and an empirical repeatability study to establish the minimal detectable change, true longitudinal biological coupling is easily mistaken for a null finding.

Part 3 · When Adjustment Meets Measurement Error

Why Naive Regression (Brain ~ Retina + Age) Creates the Impression of “No Association”

Now put Part 1 and Part 2 together—because this is what happens in almost every real retinal biomarker paper. Suppose increasing Age (C*) contributes to shared microvascular disease (U*) and also has some direct non-vascular effects on the brain. A researcher fits a standard multiple regression of Brain WMH (B) on Retinal Biomarker (R) and Age (C) to test whether the retina reflects brain microvascular pathology beyond chronological age.

Look at the severe measurement asymmetry between the two predictors in B ~ R + C:

  • Chronological Age (C) is known with essentially zero error (σϵC ≈ 0, ICCC = 1.00).
  • The Retinal Biomarker (R) and Brain MRI (B) are measured with physiological and technical noise (ICCR < 1, ICCB < 1).

When you adjust for exact Age (C)—which, by the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell (FWL) theorem, is identical to plotting the residuals of B against the residuals of R after removing C—you strip away the age-related portion of the true biological signal in R*, while leaving 100% of the retinal measurement noise ϵR behind. Even when Age affects the brain purely by causing microvascular disease (C* → U* → {R*, B*}, with direct path α = 0), dialling retinal reliability down to ICCR ≈ 0.30 flattens the FWL residual plot, kills the adjusted retina slope, and transfers the vascular effect onto Age!

Conversely, if you know the measurement error variance from a repeatability study (e.g., repeated scans in n = 20–30 participants), an Errors-in-Variables (EIV) / SEM model reverses both biases—restoring the retina slope and shrinking the inflated Age slope—while honestly widening confidence intervals to reflect the cost of noise.

Optional Deeper Dive · Why the FWL residual plot fails, effect transfer onto Age, & R (lavaan) code

By the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell (FWL) theorem, the multiple regression coefficient on R in B ~ R + C is identical to the simple slope of residualised Bres on residualised Rres (left plot above). Suppose true retinal biology R* correlates with exact Age C at ρ = Corr(R*, C), and observed R = R* + ēR has marginal reliability ICCR. Residualising R on exact Age subtracts ρ2 of the true biological variance from the horizontal axis while removing none of the random measurement noise, driving conditional reliability down to:

ICCR | C = (1 − ρ2) · ICCR 1 − ρ2 · ICCR < ICCR,   E[β̂OLS, C] − βtrue, C = Cov(R*, C) Var(C) · βtrue, R · (1 − ICCR | C)

Notice the second equation: every unit of slope that noisy R loses (1 − ICCR|C) is systematically transferred onto exact Age (C) in proportion to the regression of R* on C! At ICCR = 0.30 (Preset 3A), conditional reliability collapses to ICCR | C = 0.17, wiping out >80% of the retinal slope and inflating the Age coefficient ~4.5-fold. In R (lavaan), you can account for measurement error either using an external within-subject repeatability variance (Option A) or directly from replicate measurements within the cohort (Option B, which also propagates uncertainty in the error variance automatically): 

library(lavaan)

# OPTION A: Using within-subject error variance sigma2_w from a repeatability study
# (Prefer transporting within-subject variance sigma2_w rather than ICC, since ICC
# depends on between-subject spread in the sample.)
eiv_external <- '
  R_true =~ 1 * R_obs
  R_obs  ~~ PRIOR_ERR * R_obs   # fix to empirical within-subject error variance (sigma2_w / k)
  WMH ~ beta_retina * R_true + beta_age * Age
  R_true ~ Age
'
fit_a <- sem(sub("PRIOR_ERR", 0.42, eiv_external), data = df)

# OPTION B: Even better — if >=2 replicate scans (R_1, R_2) are collected in the cohort,
# estimate latent R_true and error variance jointly with full uncertainty propagation:
eiv_replicates <- '
  R_true =~ 1 * R_1 + 1 * R_2
  R_1 ~~ evar * R_1
  R_2 ~~ evar * R_2
  WMH ~ beta_retina * R_true + beta_age * Age
  R_true ~ Age
'
fit_b <- sem(eiv_replicates, data = df)
summary(fit_b, ci = TRUE, standardized = TRUE)
Part 4 · Engineering & Analytical Specification

Designing—and Analysing—an Ideal Retinal Biomarker

Statistical disattenuation rescues bias, but it cannot manufacture information out of pure noise—as you saw in Part 3, when SNR is very low, honest disattenuated confidence intervals become too wide to be clinically useful in realistic sample sizes.

Conversely, why not simply scan everyone’s brain directly with dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI or PET? Because a biomarker’s real-world impact depends on an engineering specification that balances biological proximity and measurement physics against speed, cost, convenience, availability, and invasiveness. Even an ideal, zero-noise retinal biomarker still requires causal judgment: If a next-generation device achieves ICCR = 0.98, its association with brain disease can still be wiped out if an analyst conditions on a direct marker of the sole common cause (R* ← Disease → B*, Preset 3D), or distorted if the study selects patients on a downstream collider (such as hospital admission). High SNR and explicit DAG thinking are complements, never substitutes.

Practical Take-Home Checklist for Retinal Biomarker Studies

Step 1 · Graph First

Draw the SEM Before Running Regression

State explicitly what latent process (U*) you think links the eye and brain, which observed variables have measurement error, and whether each candidate covariate blocks a non-target fork, screens off your target biology, or opens a selection collider.

Step 2 · Quantify Noise

Measure Repeatability & Average Within-Eye

Run a dedicated test–retest repeatability study (n ≈ 15–30) and check fellow-eye consistency before interpreting clinical regressions. Where physics allows, average k repeated cycles or frames within an eye to shrink σϵR2 / k.

Step 3 · Adjust Specifics

Control Specific Ocular Confounders

Rather than relying on chronological age alone as a blunt, error-free proxy that steals shared vascular signal, measure and adjust directly for local ocular/physiological confounders (e.g., media opacity, image quality, OCT layer thickness, acute blood pressure).

Step 4 · Model the Error

Disattenuate (or Use Within-Person Trial Designs)

In observational mechanistic studies, plug empirical repeatability estimates into an Errors-in-Variables / SEM model so noisy retinal markers compete fairly against exact covariates. In short randomised trials, leverage within-person change (ΔAge = 0) paired with high within-visit SNR.
